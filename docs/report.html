Walking a building blind, and landing within half a metre

A phone in airplane mode has three things it can still use: the way its body accelerates, the way the building's steel bends the Earth's magnetic field, and the fact that people do not walk through walls. Fused in a particle filter, those three close a 96-metre loop with 0.51 m of error — against 13.35 m for step counting alone.

Error does not accumulate — that is the whole point

This is the single most important chart in the report. Dead-reckoning error grows without bound, because every step adds a little heading error and nothing ever removes it. The fused estimate is bounded: each time the phone re-reads the magnetic field it is corrected back onto the map, so the error at metre 96 looks like the error at metre 10.

What each layer is worth

Turning the components on one at a time separates the contributions. Two results are worth stopping on.

Counter-intuitive result

The particle filter on its own is worse than the raw step counter — 7.81 m against 5.08 m. A filter adds process noise; without an observation to correct against, that noise is all it adds. The filter is not the invention here. The observations are: the magnetic map and the wall geometry. This is worth saying out loud before a judge says it for you.

Figure 4 & 5

The tail, and whether the error bars are honest

Average error is the number people quote and the least useful one. What decides whether a firefighter finds the door is the tail — how bad it gets in the worst few percent of moments — and whether the system's own confidence can be trusted when it matters.

Reference

What each number actually means

Positioning accuracy is quoted in at least six incompatible ways, and the flattering one is usually the one on the slide. These are the definitions used throughout this report.

Loop-closure error

metres

Start and finish at the same physical point; the error is the straight-line distance between where the system says you ended and where you actually started.

It is the field metric because it needs no survey equipment at all — a piece of tape on the floor is the whole apparatus. It is also the one a judge can verify by watching.

fused 0.51 m  ·  dead reckoning 13.35 m

Drift

per cent of distance walked

Loop-closure error divided by the length of the walk. The standard quality figure for dead reckoning, because closure error on its own means nothing until you know how far you went to earn it.

Published smartphone dead reckoning typically lands in the 5–15% band. Ours sits there too — until the maps are switched on.

fused 0.5%  ·  dead reckoning 13.9%

RMSE

metres, root mean square

Error measured at every step against ground truth, squared, averaged, square-rooted. Squaring means one large excursion counts far more than several small ones.

Use it to compare algorithms. Do not use it to promise a user anything — it hides the tail.

fused 0.57 m  ·  dead reckoning 5.08 m

CEP50 / CEP95

metres, circular error probable

The radius of the circle containing half — or 95% — of all position fixes. CEP50 is the typical moment; CEP95 is the one that matters operationally, because it describes the bad moments rather than averaging them away.

A door is 0.9 m wide. CEP95 is the number that says whether you walk through it or into the frame beside it.

fused 0.45 / 1.02 m  ·  dead reckoning 3.75 / 11.03 m

1 σ radius

metres, reported live

The particle cloud has a covariance; this is the semi-major axis of its one-sigma ellipse. The app draws it as a ring and the user sees it grow when the system loses confidence.

Careful with this one: for a two-dimensional radial error, 1 σ is expected to contain 39.3% of fixes (Rayleigh), not the 68% people remember from one-dimensional statistics. 68% sits at roughly 1.51 σ. Quoting 68% here is a real and common mistake.

mean reported radius 0.98 m

Coverage

per cent, observed vs. expected

Of all the fixes, how many had their true position inside the circle the filter claimed? Compared against the Rayleigh expectation, this says whether the error bars are honest.

Under-coverage means the system is overconfident, which is dangerous. Over-coverage means it is cautious, which costs precision but not safety. Ours is cautious.

at 1 σ: observed 57.7% vs. expected 39.3%

Honesty

What these numbers are, and what they are not

Every figure on this page comes from synthetic walks — simulated sensor streams with ground truth known exactly. That is a deliberate choice and it has a specific scope.

  • What it proves. The algorithm is correct, the fusion works, the parameters are in a sane region, and the error bars are calibrated. It also proves the team can iterate in seconds instead of walking a corridor for every parameter change.
  • What it does not prove. Real magnetic fields are less smooth than the model. Real people hold the phone at an angle, put it in a pocket, and change pace. Real gyroscope bias drifts with temperature. Expect real-world numbers to be worse — the honest expectation is CEP50 in the 1–2 m range, not 0.45 m.
  • What is modelled. Gyroscope bias at 0.008 rad/s (28°/min — realistic for a phone MEMS), sensor noise on all three axes, a magnetic anomaly field of ±18 µT, walking cadence and vertical bob, and pauses at each corner.
  • What is not modelled. Phone orientation changing in the hand, magnetic field drift over days, multiple floors, other people's ferrous mass moving through the space.
  • Before the pitch: replace this page's numbers with a real corridor walk. The method stays; only the data changes. That is the point of building the measurement harness first.

One structural guard worth naming: the magnetic map was built from walk #7 and scored on walks #11, #23, #37, #41 and #53. Without that split the numbers would measure the algorithm's ability to memorise noise rather than to locate anything.
